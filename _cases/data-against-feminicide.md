# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "Data Against Feminicide (DCF) is a South-North\
  \ participatory action research and design project that supports the work of activists\
  \ and civil society organizations that produce data on gender-based violence and\
  \ its most lethal expression: feminicide, broadly understood as the gender-related\
  \ killing of cisgender and transgender women and girls.\nFeminicide is a structural\
  \ problem worldwide, but official statistics consistently underestimate its scale\
  \ due to issues such as underreporting and misclassification. In contrast, many\
  \ civil society groups produce their own data to hold public institutions accountable,\
  \ support collective action, and remember lives lost. As our research has shown,\
  \ these groups often rely on news media to identify and monitor cases over time,\
  \ work that is both labor-intensive and emotionally taxing.\nOver the past seven\
  \ years, our team has used participatory methods to work alongside 13 groups across\
  \ North and South America and Sub-Saharan Africa to co-design two digital tools\
  \ to support this monitoring. The first tool is a browser extension (the Data Against\
  \ Feminicide Highlighter) that helps activists extract and record information from\
  \ news articles more efficiently. The second is an intelligent notification system\
  \ (Data Against Feminicide Email Alerts) that uses machine learning (ML) to scan\
  \ news sources, identify articles that likely concern a feminicide case, and deliver\
  \ them directly to activists\u2019 inboxes. The ML model was trained through collaborative\
  \ data annotation with civil society partners, grounding it in activists' own contextual\
  \ knowledge. Both tools are available in English, Spanish, Portuguese, and Swahili.\
  \ The Highlighter plug-in has over 220 users and more than 65 activists and organizations\
  \ across 16 countries use the AI-powered Email Alerts system.\nThe tools aim to\
  \ reduce the burden of manual data collection without replacing activist knowledge\
  \ and judgment. Verification, interpretation, and decision-making remain in activists\u2019\
  \ hands \u2014 as does ownership of the data, which stays with the organizations\
  \ that produce it. \nDCF is currently collaborating with the DISCO Lab at Brown\
  \ University and with a number of activists and journalists \u2013 mainly from Latin\
  \ America \u2013 on a new co-design project exploring the potential of AI tools\
  \ to support more responsible news reporting on feminicide. "
# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: 'News media coverage of feminicide
  is uneven and reflects existing biases. Outlets tend to report less frequently on
  Black women, Indigenous women, women in rural areas. LGBTQ+ people and transgender
  women and girls are also often misgendered in news reports. These gaps carry through
  into training data and may be reproduced in model outputs. During participatory
  evaluation processes, partner organizations whose work focused on racialized feminicide
  found that the tools performed significantly worse for them than for groups monitoring
  all types of feminicide. This made clear that a single general model was inadequate,
  and that detecting cases involving specific communities required separately trained
  models. This finding shaped our subsequent research into more finely tuned models
  for specific types of gender-based violence (Suresh et al. 2022, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3531146.3533132).

  Additionally, the project''s technical infrastructure, including the servers hosting
  the tools and the software development team, has remained based in Global North
  institutions. This risks producing uneven resources and reproducing colonial dynamics.
  In a recent collaboration with Femicide Count Kenya, the team sought to address
  this by not only co-designing tools with the organization but also building the
  capacity of a local software developer throughout the process. This was time-consuming
  but ultimately resulted in a strong relationship between the organization and the
  developer. We hope to continue to experiment with decentralizing technical expertise
  and infrastructure and build further local capacity for activist communities to
  work with AI. '

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: "Recruitment varied across the different phases of tool development.\
  \ For the initial English and Spanish models, we organized an open online event\
  \ and invited potential participants drawing on a list of feminicide monitoring\
  \ projects originally compiled by Feminicidio Uruguay (one of the project partners)\
  \ and expanded through further research by the Data + Feminism Lab, as well as on\
  \ academic and activist networks built by the DCF team over time. For the Portuguese\
  \ and Swahili iterations, recruitment drew on relationships built through the project's\
  \ own qualitative research: groups that had participated in interviews or informal\
  \ conversations about their data practices were approached directly about collaborating\
  \ to expand the tools into their language and context. Each iteration involved a\
  \ distinct participatory process with local partners rather than a single uniform\
  \ approach.\nAccessibility and inclusion were supported through multilingual engagement.\
  \ For each process, we prepared language-specific materials adapted to local contexts.\
  \ The participatory processes were also conducted in the native language of participants\
  \ and facilitated by fluent team members with relevant contextual experience. Participants\
  \ in the co-development process were compensated for their time and knowledge, recognizing\
  \ both the value of their contributions and the practical barriers that can limit\
  \ participation in research processes.\n\nThe following civil society groups participated\
  \ in the process: Feminicidio Uruguay; Sovereign Bodies Institute - SBI; African\
  \ American Policy Forum; Women Count USA; Black Femicide US; Observatorio Nacional\
  \ MuMaL\xE1; Mujeres de Negro Rosario; N\xE9ias - Observat\xF3rio de Feminic\xED\
  dios Londrina; F\xF3rum Cearense de Mulheres - FCM; Levante Feminista Contra o Feminic\xED\
  dio, Lesboc\xEDdio e Transfeminic\xEDdio; Lupa Feminista contra o Feminic\xEDdio\
  \ - Coletivo Feminino Plural; Laborat\xF3rio de Estudos de Feminic\xEDdios - LESFEM;\
  \ Grupo de Trabalho sobre Feminic\xEDdio na Bahia - GT FEM; Femicide Count Kenya.\n\
  \nThe tools themselves were designed to be low-tech on the user side. After the\
  \ initial setup, the Email Alerts system delivers filtered news articles by email,\
  \ and the Data Highlighter marks up words on a webpage. Activists record cases however\
  \ they see fit, whether in a sophisticated database or a paper notebook. The system\
  \ imposes no particular data schema, which means it can be used by a wide range\
  \ of organizations and activists regardless of their technical capacity. Activists\
  \ own and store their own data separately; the DCF team has no access to it. We\
  \ maintain language-specific WhatsApp communities for activists using the tools,\
  \ providing technical support, facilitating knowledge exchange, and sustaining a\
  \ broader community of practice around feminicide data production."

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: 'DCF offers a distinctive example
  of participatory and responsible AI development grounded in feminist principles
  and community-centered design. Several features make it important to include in
  the case book.

  First, the project engages communities that are often left out of AI governance
  discussions: feminist activists, grassroots organizations, and groups documenting
  gender-based violence across Latin America, North America, and Sub-Saharan Africa.
  Their expertise shaped every stage of tool development, from defining what counts
  as a feminicide case to annotating training data and evaluating outputs.

  Second, the tools were built not only for communities but with them. Participatory
  workshops and collaborative design processes ensured the technologies reflected
  activists'' existing workflows, political priorities, and local knowledge. This
  challenges the more common pattern in AI development, where tools are designed without
  meaningful input from the people most affected and most participation amounts to
  no more than consultation, as recent research on participatory AI has shown.

  Third, the project takes seriously the limits and risks of AI. Rather than automating
  activist work, the tools are designed to reduce repetitive and emotionally harmful
  labor while keeping human judgment central. The project also directly addresses
  questions of bias and erasure in AI systems, including how media and institutional
  data can misrepresent or render invisible certain victims and communities.

  Finally, DCF connects public engagement to wider questions of data governance, accountability,
  and power. Each language iteration of the tools has involved a distinct co-design
  process, attentive to the situated knowledge and working conditions of the groups
  involved. This makes it a useful model for thinking about how AI development can
  be made more accountable to the communities it is meant to serve.'
