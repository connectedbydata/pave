# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: 'In Kalinga, in the Cordillera highlands
  of the northern Philippines, an arang is the open ground where the community gathers
  to decide things together. The Digital Arang borrowed that gathering and asked a
  new question: who decides how data and AI may touch Indigenous knowledge, and on
  what terms? Working with Kalinga State University, the Indigenous Peoples'' Mandatory
  Representation, the National Commission on Indigenous Peoples, and community elders,
  the project ran a series of governance workshops that produced an Indigenous Knowledge,
  Systems and Practices (IKSP) framework and a local AI Policy Ordinance. The ordinance
  translates the principle of free, prior and informed consent into concrete rules
  for how Indigenous data may be collected, stored, accessed, and refused. It treats
  opacity, the community''s right not to be made legible, as a governance choice rather
  than a gap to be filled.'
# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: 'Candidates: the engagement centred

  Kalinga communities; timeframes were compressed by fieldwork logistics; the ordinance''s
  enforcement depends on local and national mechanisms still being established.'

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: The project used the community's own deliberative form, rather
  than imposing an external workshop template, so that authority and pacing stayed
  with the elders. Recruitment ran through Indigenous representative structures (IPMR,
  NCIP) rather than individual sign-up. Responsible-AI training was provided first
  so participants engaged the governance questions from an informed position rather
  than being asked to react cold.

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: Most participatory AI work in this
  collection comes from North America and Europe and asks how to consult citizens
  about AI systems built elsewhere. This case starts from a different place. It centres
  an Indigenous community setting binding terms on whether and how AI may engage their
  knowledge at all. The contribution is a working model for data and AI governance
  grounded in Indigenous self-determination and FPIC, developed in the community's
  own deliberative form rather than imported from a citizens-assembly template. It
  engages a community almost entirely absent from global AI governance and shows that
  the right to refuse is itself a governable principle.
